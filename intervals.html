Intervals

Measure the distance between notes

An interval describes how far apart two notes are. When you can hear and find that distance on the fretboard, it’s easier to work out riffs and choose notes that fit a chord.

  • Count the letter names
  • Count the semitones
  • Spell the interval
  • Invert an interval
  • Practice
  • Reference diagrams

Count the letter names

Start by finding the interval’s number. Count both note names and every letter between them. C to E is a third because C, D, E makes three letters.

C to E♭ is also a third. Sharps and flats change how large it sounds, while the letter names determine the number.

Count the semitones

Then find the interval’s quality. On one bass string, count one semitone for each fret you move.

  • C to E: 4 semitones, a major third.
  • C to E♭: 3 semitones, a minor third.
  • C to G: 7 semitones, a perfect fifth.

Seconds, thirds, sixths, and sevenths come in major or minor forms. Unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves have a perfect form. When you stretch a major or perfect interval by a semitone, it becomes augmented. Shrink a minor or perfect interval by a semitone and it becomes diminished.

Spell the interval

To write a minor third above D, count D–E–F first. D to F spans three semitones, so F is already the right spelling. For a major third, raise that F to F♯.

On a fretted bass F♯ and G♭ sound at the same pitch, but D to G♭ counts as a fourth. Keeping the letter names straight makes chords and scales easier to read.

Invert an interval

Move the lower note up an octave so it becomes the higher note. For a simple interval and its inversion, the two interval numbers add up to nine.

  • C up to E is a major third. E up to C is a minor sixth.
  • C up to F is a perfect fourth. F up to C is a perfect fifth.

Major and minor swap, augmented and diminished swap, and perfect stays perfect.

Hear the difference one fret makes

  1. On the A string, play C (fret 3), then E (fret 7).
  2. Play C again, then E♭ (fret 6).
  3. Alternate those two sounds a few times. Say “major third” or “minor third” as you play them.

Try next: From the same C, play G (fret 10) for a perfect fifth and the next C (fret 15) for an octave.
