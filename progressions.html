Chord progressions

Build a bass line through chord changes

A chord progression is a sequence of chords. Start by playing each chord’s root on the first beat, then decide where to leave space and where to lead into the next chord.

  • Read Roman numerals
  • Play common progressions
  • Connect chord tones
  • Hear how phrases end
  • Follow a circle progression
  • Use inversions for smoother lines
  • Practice
  • Reference diagrams

Read Roman numerals

A Roman numeral gives the scale degree of a chord’s root. In C major, I starts on C, IV on F, and V on G. Uppercase usually means major, lowercase means minor, and the small circle in vii° means diminished.

The same pattern works in another key. I–IV–V is C–F–G in C major and G–C–D in G major.

Play common progressions

Try these examples in C major with one bar per chord:

  • I–IV–V: C–F–G. Hear it in rock, folk, and many blues forms.
  • ii–V–I: Dm–G–C. A familiar pattern in jazz.
  • I–vi–IV–V: C–Am–F–G. Common in doo-wop and pop.
  • I–V–vi–IV: C–G–Am–F. A common pop loop.

Connect chord tones

A non-chord tone is a note outside the chord that’s sounding. A passing tone fills a gap between two chord tones, while a neighbor tone steps away and returns.

Over a C major chord, play C–D–E to hear D pass between chord tones. Then try C–D–C to hear D as a neighbor. Other common non-chord tones include suspensions and appoggiaturas.

Hear how phrases end

A cadence acts like punctuation at the end of a musical phrase. Sing or play the bass roots to hear where each version lands.

  • Authentic: V–I sounds like coming home.
  • Plagal: IV–I is the familiar “Amen” motion.
  • Half: An ending on V leaves the phrase open.
  • Deceptive: V–vi replaces the expected I with a different chord.

Follow a circle progression

Circle progressions generally move the root down a fifth or up a fourth. The diatonic version stays in one key: I–IV–vii°–iii–vi–ii–V–I.

In C major the roots are C–F–B–E–A–D–G–C. F to B is a tritone, the one move in this sequence that keeps the pattern inside the key while departing from perfect fourths and fifths.

Use inversions for smoother lines

In a first inversion the third is in the bass, as in C/E. In a second inversion the fifth is in the bass, as in C/G. You may see those positions written as I6 and I64 in theory examples.

Play the bass notes C–B–A for C–G/B–Am to hear how an inversion fills in the step between C and A. In a classical cadential 6/4, you can also hear C/G lead to G, then C. The bass holds G across the first two chords.

Make a four-bar bass line

  1. Loop C–G–Am–F, one chord per bar in 4/4. Play only the root on beat 1 of each bar.
  2. On the repeat, play the fifth on beat 3. The fifths are G, D, E, and C.
  3. Try G/B instead of G. Listen to the bass line step C–B–A before moving to F.

Listen for: A clear chord change on beat 1. Keep the beat steady as you add notes.
